What a mortgage really is

A mortgage is a loan secured by real estate. You borrow money to buy, refinance, or access equity in a property, and the lender registers a legal charge against that property until the loan is paid, transferred, discharged, or replaced.

Most clients focus first on the interest rate. The better starting point is the full structure: loan amount, down payment, amortization, term, payment frequency, prepayment flexibility, penalties, lender conditions, closing costs, and the plan for renewal.

Three numbers shape most mortgage decisions

  • Purchase price or property value: The price being paid for a purchase, or the estimated market value when refinancing.
  • Mortgage amount: The amount borrowed after down payment, equity, insurance premium, refinance funds, or debt consolidation are factored in.
  • Payment comfort: The amount your household can manage after mortgage payment, property tax, utilities, insurance, maintenance, and normal living costs.

Buying Process

How the mortgage process works

  1. Prepare your file

    Before shopping seriously, organize income documents, down payment history, debt information, credit details, and an honest monthly budget. A stronger file creates more lender options.

    • Employment letter, pay stubs, T4s, NOAs, business financials if self-employed.
    • Bank statements showing down payment source and deposit history.
    • Details for debts, loans, leases, support payments, and existing properties.

  2. Get pre-approved

    A pre-approval estimates how much you may qualify for before you make an offer. It can include a rate hold, but it is not a final approval because the property and updated documents still need lender review.

  3. Make an offer with the right conditions

    When possible, protect yourself with financing, inspection, insurance, and legal review conditions. The right conditions give time to confirm the property and mortgage approval.

  4. Submit the live deal

    The lender reviews the borrower, property, down payment, appraisal if needed, and purchase contract. They may issue a commitment with conditions that must be satisfied before closing.

  5. Close with your lawyer

    Your lawyer or notary registers the mortgage, handles title, receives lender funds, collects your remaining down payment and closing costs, and completes the property transfer.

Qualification

How lenders decide what you can borrow

Mortgage qualification is a risk review. Lenders look at whether you can repay the mortgage, whether the property is acceptable security, and whether the full story makes sense.

The lender looks at five major areas

  • Income: Salary, hourly income, overtime, bonus, commission, pension, business income, rental income, or other accepted sources.
  • Credit: Score, repayment history, credit limits, late payments, collections, bankruptcies, consumer proposals, and overall borrowing habits.
  • Debt: Credit cards, lines of credit, car loans, leases, student loans, support payments, existing mortgages, and property costs.
  • Down payment or equity: Amount, source, history, gift letter if applicable, and whether the funds are eligible for the program.
  • Property: Location, condition, value, type, zoning, marketability, appraisal, taxes, condo fees, and intended use.

Debt-service ratios in plain language

GDS
Gross Debt Service compares housing costs to gross income. Housing costs usually include mortgage payment, property tax, heating, and 50% of condo fees if applicable.
TDS
Total Debt Service compares housing costs plus other debt payments to gross income.
Common guideposts
FCAC describes 39% for GDS and 44% for TDS as common affordability limits. Some lenders and products may use different rules.

Stress test

For federally regulated lenders, borrowers are generally tested at a qualifying rate higher than the contract rate. OSFI lists the current uninsured mortgage qualifying rate as the greater of the contract rate plus 2% or 5.25%. This helps test whether the borrower could handle higher payments or financial pressure.

Down Payment

Down payment rules and mortgage insurance

Your down payment affects the mortgage amount, insurance requirement, lender options, and closing cash needed. In Canada, the minimum down payment depends on the purchase price.

Purchase Price Minimum Down Payment Common Insurance Result
$500,000 or less 5% of the purchase price Mortgage loan insurance is typically required if down payment is under 20%.
More than $500,000 and less than $1.5 million 5% on the first $500,000, plus 10% on the amount above $500,000 Insurance is typically required if down payment is under 20%, subject to insurer and lender rules.
$1.5 million or more 20% of the purchase price Generally uninsured because the minimum down payment is 20%.

Where down payment can come from

  • Personal savings or investments.
  • Gift from eligible family, usually with a signed gift letter.
  • RRSP Home Buyers' Plan funds if eligible.
  • Sale of another property.
  • Borrowed funds only if the lender allows them and includes the repayment in qualification.

Mortgage loan insurance

Mortgage loan insurance is usually required when the down payment is less than 20%. It protects the lender if the borrower defaults, but it can help buyers qualify with a smaller down payment. The premium is commonly added to the mortgage amount, which increases the loan and payment.

Mortgage Types

Choosing the right mortgage structure

The best mortgage is not always the lowest posted rate. The right structure depends on how long you expect to keep the property, whether you may move, whether income is stable, how much payment risk you can handle, and whether you plan to make extra payments.

Fixed vs. variable

Fixed rate
The rate stays the same for the term. This gives payment predictability and easier budgeting.
Variable rate
The rate can move with the lender's prime rate. Payments may change, or the payment may stay fixed while the principal-interest split changes, depending on the product.

Open vs. closed

Open mortgage
More flexible for paying down or paying off the mortgage, usually at a higher interest rate.
Closed mortgage
Usually lower rate than open, but extra payments are limited by prepayment privileges and penalties may apply if broken early.

Insured, insurable, uninsured

Insured
Mortgage loan insurance is paid because the down payment is below 20%.
Insurable
The borrower has 20% or more down, but the file still meets insurer-style criteria.
Uninsured
No mortgage loan insurance. Common with 20% or more down, refinances, higher-value purchases, or products outside insurer rules.

Special mortgage and lending scenarios

  • HELOC: A revolving home equity line of credit secured by real estate. Useful for flexible borrowing, but it can create long-term debt if not managed carefully.
  • Second mortgage: A mortgage registered behind the first mortgage. Often higher rate because the lender takes more risk.
  • Bridge financing: Short-term financing that helps when a new purchase closes before the sale of the existing home closes.
  • Construction financing: Funds are usually released in stages as construction progresses and inspections or appraisals confirm progress.
  • Commercial mortgage: Financing for commercial or mixed-use properties, often underwritten with more focus on property income, business strength, and risk.
  • Reverse mortgage: A loan secured against home equity, typically for older homeowners, where repayment is usually deferred until sale, move, or death. Suitability and cost should be reviewed carefully.

Rates & Payments

What affects your mortgage payment

Your payment is shaped by the amount borrowed, interest rate, amortization, payment frequency, and whether mortgage insurance or fees are added to the loan.

Mortgage Payment Loan Amount Interest Rate Amortization Payment Frequency Insurance Premium

Term vs. amortization

  • Term: The length of your current mortgage contract. Common terms range from a few months to five years or longer.
  • Amortization: The total time planned to repay the mortgage in full. Longer amortization lowers the payment but increases total interest over time.
  • Renewal: At the end of each term, you renew, switch lenders, refinance, or pay off the balance.

Payment frequency

Monthly, semi-monthly, bi-weekly, accelerated bi-weekly, weekly, and accelerated weekly payments can affect budgeting and interest over time. Accelerated options may reduce the balance faster because you pay a little more each year.

Costs

Costs to plan for beyond the down payment

Many buyers budget for the down payment but forget cash needed at closing and after moving in. Keep a separate cushion for closing costs, moving costs, repairs, and the first few months of ownership.

  • Land transfer tax: A provincial tax paid on closing. Some cities may have additional municipal land transfer tax. First-time buyer rebates may apply if eligible.
  • Legal fees and disbursements: Lawyer or notary work for title, registration, searches, adjustments, and closing.
  • Title insurance: Often arranged through the lawyer to protect against certain title defects, fraud, or registration issues.
  • Home inspection: Optional but useful for understanding the property's condition before firming up an offer.
  • Appraisal: A lender may require an appraisal to confirm property value and marketability.
  • Property tax and utility adjustments: The buyer may reimburse the seller for prepaid costs from closing date onward.
  • Insurance: Lenders usually require proof of property insurance before closing.
  • Moving and setup: Movers, utility deposits, immediate repairs, furnishings, locks, and maintenance tools.

Renewal & Refinance

What to do when you already own the property

Mortgage renewal

Renewal happens when your mortgage term ends. You can accept your current lender's offer, negotiate, switch lenders, change term, change payment frequency, or adjust strategy. Do not treat the first renewal letter as the final answer.

  • Start reviewing options a few months before maturity.
  • Compare rate, payment, prepayment privileges, penalties, portability, and service.
  • Ask whether switching lenders creates setup, discharge, appraisal, legal, or registration costs.
  • If you already have mortgage loan insurance, ask for the insurance certificate details before switching.

Refinance

Refinancing means replacing or changing the mortgage before or at maturity, usually to access equity, consolidate debt, change amortization, remove/add a borrower, or improve structure. A refinance normally requires qualification and may involve appraisal, legal work, and penalties if done before the term ends.

Prepayment penalties

A lender may charge a penalty if you break a closed mortgage, pay more than allowed, transfer the mortgage before the term ends, or repay the full balance early. Open mortgages usually allow repayment without penalty but often carry higher rates.

Working With a Broker

What a licensed mortgage professional should help you understand

In Ontario, mortgage brokerages, brokers, agents, and administrators are regulated by FSRA unless exempt. A licensed mortgage professional can compare lender options, collect documents, explain terms and risks, and guide the file through approval and closing.

Questions to ask before you proceed

  • Are you licensed with FSRA, and what is your licence level?
  • Who do you represent in this transaction: me, the lender, or both?
  • How are you paid, and are there any broker or lender fees?
  • Which lenders are being considered, and why?
  • What are the risks, penalties, restrictions, and total cost of borrowing?
  • What documents do you need, and how will my information be used?
  • What conditions must be satisfied before closing?

Documents clients are often asked for

  • Government ID
  • Employment letter
  • Recent pay stubs
  • T4s and notices of assessment
  • Business financials if self-employed
  • Bank statements for down payment
  • Gift letter if applicable
  • Purchase agreement and MLS listing
  • Property tax bill
  • Mortgage statement for refinance or switch
  • Condo status certificate if applicable
  • Lawyer contact information

Glossary

Mortgage terms explained clearly

Amortization
The total repayment period used to calculate mortgage payments. A longer amortization lowers payments but increases total interest.
Appraisal
A professional estimate of property value ordered by the lender or broker to support the mortgage amount.
APR
Annual percentage rate. A broader annual cost measure that can include interest and certain borrowing costs.
Assumable Mortgage
A mortgage that may be transferred to a buyer if the lender and contract allow it.
Bridge Financing
Short-term financing used when buying a new property before the sale proceeds from an existing property are available.
Closed Mortgage
A mortgage with limits on extra payments and early payout. Breaking it can trigger a penalty.
Closing Costs
Costs due at closing beyond down payment, such as land transfer tax, legal fees, title insurance, adjustments, appraisal, and insurance.
Collateral Charge
A mortgage registration type that may secure more than the current mortgage balance and can affect switching costs.
Commitment Letter
The lender's written approval terms, including rate, amount, payment, conditions, and expiry.
Conventional Mortgage
A mortgage with 20% or more equity/down payment. It is usually uninsured.
Debt Consolidation
Using mortgage financing to pay out higher-interest debts. It may lower monthly payments but can extend debt over a longer period.
Down Payment
The buyer's own contribution toward the purchase price. The mortgage covers the remaining eligible amount.
Equity
The difference between market value and debts secured against the property.
Fixed Rate
An interest rate that stays the same for the mortgage term.
GDS Ratio
Gross Debt Service ratio. Housing costs divided by gross income.
HELOC
Home equity line of credit. A revolving credit line secured by real estate.
High-Ratio Mortgage
A mortgage with less than 20% down payment, usually requiring mortgage loan insurance.
Interest Adjustment Date
The date interest is adjusted before regular mortgage payments begin.
Land Transfer Tax
A tax paid by the buyer when property ownership transfers.
LTV Ratio
Loan-to-value ratio. Mortgage amount divided by property value or purchase price.
Maturity Date
The end date of the current mortgage term.
Mortgage Default Insurance
Insurance protecting the lender when a borrower has a smaller down payment. The borrower usually pays the premium.
Mortgage Term
The length of the mortgage contract, including rate, payment, and conditions.
Open Mortgage
A mortgage that allows extra payments or full payout without penalty, usually at a higher rate.
Portability
A feature that may allow you to transfer a mortgage to another property, subject to lender approval.
Pre-Approval
A preliminary review that estimates borrowing power before a live property approval.
Prepayment Privilege
The amount you can pay above regular payments without penalty.
Prime Rate
A lender's reference rate used for variable-rate mortgages and lines of credit.
Refinance
Changing or replacing a mortgage, often to access equity, consolidate debt, or change structure.
Renewal
Choosing a new term when the current mortgage term ends.
Second Mortgage
A mortgage registered behind the first mortgage. It usually has a higher rate due to higher lender risk.
Stress Test
A qualification test using a higher interest rate than the contract rate to assess affordability under pressure.
TDS Ratio
Total Debt Service ratio. Housing costs plus other debt payments divided by gross income.
Title Insurance
Insurance that protects against certain title and registration problems.
Trigger Rate
For some variable-rate mortgages, the rate where the payment no longer covers all interest due.
Variable Rate
A mortgage rate that can change when the lender's prime rate changes.
